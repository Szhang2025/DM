Math & Stats Foundations

Every algorithm above is really just one of a handful of recurring math/stats ideas, applied to data. You don't need a heavy math background to get the gist of any of them — each topic below starts with a plain-language idea (no formulas required), then a tiny worked example with real numbers so you can see exactly what's being computed. Click a topic to expand it.

Maximum Likelihood Estimation — "which explanation makes what I saw most believable?"

Imagine flipping a coin 10 times and getting 7 heads. What's the most believable value for that coin's true probability of heads? Maximum likelihood says: pick the probability that makes the data you actually observed as likely as possible. Here that's 0.7 — not because it's "correct" in some absolute sense, but because no other value explains your specific 7-heads-out-of-10 result as well.

Logistic Regression does the same thing with your training rows standing in for coin flips: each row's true label (yes/no) is treated as a coin flip whose bias is whatever the model's current weights say it should be. Maximum likelihood adjusts the weights so that the actual labels you observed become as probable as possible under the model — that adjustment is what the gradient descent step (see below) is actually doing on every iteration.

Naive Bayes uses it differently: it first applies Bayes' theorem to flip the question around — instead of estimating "probability of class, given the features" directly, it estimates "probability of the features, given each class," then combines that with how common each class is overall. Maximum likelihood is how it estimates those per-class feature distributions (for Gaussian Naive Bayes, that just means computing each feature's mean and spread separately within each class — the mean and standard deviation you'd calculate by hand are themselves the maximum-likelihood estimates for a normal distribution).

Worked example: 7 heads, 3 tails. The likelihood of a coin with heads-probability p producing exactly this sequence is p⁷(1−p)³. Trying a few values of p:
p0.50.60.70.80.9
p⁷(1−p)³0.000980.001790.002220.001680.00048
p = 0.7 gives the highest value — confirming that the "obvious" answer (7/10 = 0.7) is also literally the maximum-likelihood answer.

Matrix Algebra & Least Squares — "the plane that comes closest to every point at once"

With one predictor, "fitting a line" means finding a slope and intercept that pass as close as possible to all your points, where "close" means minimizing the total squared vertical distance from the line to each point. With many predictors, you're doing the same thing but fitting a flat plane (or higher-dimensional equivalent) instead of a line. Matrices are just a compact, organized way to write down and solve all those equations — one per predictor and one per data row — simultaneously, rather than juggling them by hand.

The payoff is that, unlike models that need repeated trial-and-error adjustment, ordinary least squares has a direct, one-step formula for the best-fitting line/plane — no iteration required. That's what Multivariate Linear Regression in this app computes.

Worked example: three points (1, 2), (2, 3), (3, 5). Mean x̄ = 2, mean ȳ = 3.33. Least squares slope = Σ(x−x̄)(y−ȳ) / Σ(x−x̄)² = [(−1)(−1.33) + (0)(−0.33) + (1)(1.67)] / [1 + 0 + 1] = 3 / 2 = 1.5. Intercept = ȳ − slope·x̄ = 3.33 − 1.5×2 = 0.33. Best-fit line: y = 1.5x + 0.33 — the line that minimizes the total squared vertical distance to all three points. With more predictors, the same idea is solved via matrices instead of these hand sums.
Used by: Linear Regression
Impurity Measures — "how mixed-up is this group?"

A decision tree repeatedly asks yes/no questions that split the data into smaller groups. At each step, it wants to pick the question that makes each resulting group as "pure" as possible — mostly one class, or numbers that are close together. Gini index and entropy are two different ways to score how mixed-up a group is for classification: a group made entirely of one class scores 0 (no impurity at all); a 50/50 mixed group scores the highest possible value. For regression trees, there's no "class" to be pure about, so the tree instead measures variance — how spread out the numeric target values are within a group — and picks splits that shrink that spread the most.

Worked example: a bag of 10 marbles, 8 red and 2 blue (proportions 0.8 and 0.2).
Gini = 1 − (0.8² + 0.2²) = 1 − (0.64 + 0.04) = 0.32
Entropy = −(0.8·log₂0.8 + 0.2·log₂0.2) = −(−0.257 − 0.464) = 0.72 bits
Compare a 50/50 bag: Gini = 1 − (0.25+0.25) = 0.5 (the maximum), Entropy = 1.0 bit (also the maximum) — confirming both measures agree that a 50/50 mix is the most "impure" a two-class group can be.
Used by: Decision TreeRandom Forest
Bootstrap & Bagging — "many slightly-different samples from one dataset"

Imagine you have one bag of marbles (your training data) but wish you had several different bags to see how much your conclusions might vary. The bootstrap trick makes that possible from a single sample: reach in, pull out a marble, note it down, and put it back before drawing again, repeating until your new "bag" is the same size as the original. Because each marble is replaced before the next draw, some marbles get picked more than once and others don't get picked at all — but the result is a brand-new sample built entirely from your original data, without needing to collect any new data.

Bagging ("bootstrap aggregating") uses this trick to build many different training sets from one dataset, trains a separate model (typically a decision tree) on each bootstrap sample, and then combines all their predictions — majority vote for classification, averaging for regression. Since each tree sees a slightly different, randomly resampled version of the data, the trees end up different from one another; and averaging many different, individually-noisy trees together produces a more stable prediction than any single tree alone. That's exactly how Random Forest builds its 50 trees in this app — each one trained on its own bootstrap sample, with predictions combined by vote (classification) or average (regression).

Worked example: original data has 5 rows, labeled A, B, C, D, E. One bootstrap sample, drawn with replacement: [B, D, D, A, E] — notice D was picked twice, and C wasn't picked at all. That's expected: the chance a specific row is never picked across 5 draws from 5 rows is (1 − 1/5)⁵ = 0.8⁵ ≈ 0.328, so roughly 32.8% of rows are left out of any one bootstrap sample here (rows like C). As the dataset grows larger, that leftover fraction converges to about 36.8% (1/e) — those leftover, never-picked rows are called "out-of-bag" and are sometimes used as a free built-in validation set for each tree.
Used by: Random Forest
Gradient Descent — "walking downhill in the fog"

Imagine standing on a hill in thick fog, trying to reach the lowest point — the spot where your model's total error is smallest. You can't see the whole landscape, but you can feel which direction is downhill right where you're standing. Gradient descent means: take a small step in the steepest downhill direction, then feel the slope again and repeat, until you stop making progress. The "slope" at any point is called the gradient; "steepest descent" just means always stepping directly opposite the gradient — the single most efficient downhill direction — rather than some other direction.

This is genuinely how this app trains Logistic Regression and the linear SVM/SVR (the Pegasos algorithm used here is a particular flavor of gradient descent). It's also the core training method behind neural networks — this app's Neural Network options use exactly this idea, just applied once per layer via a technique called backpropagation: compute the gradient at the output layer first, then use the chain rule to push that same error signal backward through each hidden layer's weights in turn, so every layer gets its own gradient to descend. The Shallow network does this through 1 hidden layer; the Deep network does it through 2 — same "walk downhill" idea, just relayed backward through as many sets of weights as the network has layers. This is literally what makes a network "deep": nothing more mysterious than the same gradient descent idea, chained backward one extra time.

Worked example: minimize f(w) = (w−3)² starting at w = 0, with gradient 2(w−3) and a step size (learning rate) of 0.1. Each step moves opposite the gradient:

w keeps creeping toward 3 — the true minimum of f(w) — getting a little closer with every step, exactly the way training loss creeps toward its minimum over many epochs.

Distance Metrics — "how far apart are two points?"

Several algorithms need a way to answer: how similar (or different) are two data points? The most common answer is Euclidean distance — the straight-line "ruler" distance you'd get from the Pythagorean theorem, extended beyond 2 dimensions to however many features you have.

k-Nearest Neighbors uses distance directly: to classify (or predict a number for) a new point, find its k closest neighbors in the training data by distance, and let them vote (classification) or average (regression). K-Means clustering — available as its own mode in this app, since it's unsupervised and doesn't use a target column — uses the same distance idea in reverse: start with k guessed "center" points, assign every data point to its nearest center, then move each center to the average position of the points assigned to it, repeating until the centers stop moving.

Worked example: distance between points (1, 2) and (4, 6):
√[(4−1)² + (6−2)²] = √[9 + 16] = √25 = 5

Euclidean distance is actually just one member of a whole family called Minkowski distance: take the difference in each coordinate, raise it to some power p, sum those up, then take the 1/p root. Set p = 2 and you get Euclidean distance (the familiar ruler distance). Set p = 1 and you get Manhattan distance (also called "taxicab" distance — like navigating city blocks, where you can't cut diagonally through buildings). As p keeps increasing toward infinity, it converges to Chebyshev distance — just the single largest coordinate difference, ignoring all the others.

Worked example: same two points, (1, 2) and (4, 6), under different values of p:
p = 1 (Manhattan)p = 2 (Euclidean)p = ∞ (Chebyshev)
|4−1| + |6−2| = 7√(3² + 4²) = 5max(3, 4) = 4
Same two points, three different "how far apart" answers — the right choice of p depends on the problem (Manhattan is often preferred for high-dimensional or grid-like data; Euclidean is the default most people reach for first).

All of the above only works for numbers — you can't subtract "red" from "blue" and square it. When your data mixes numeric columns (age, income) with categorical ones (favorite color, job title), a common fix is Gower's distance: for each numeric column, compute the difference between the two rows and divide by that column's overall range (so every numeric column contributes a score between 0 and 1); for each categorical column, score 0 if the two rows share the same category and 1 if they differ; then average all these per-column scores into one overall distance between 0 (identical) and 1 (completely different).

Worked example: Person A is 30 years old and prefers blue; Person B is 40 and prefers red. Suppose age ranges from 20–60 across the dataset (a range of 40).
Numeric part: |30 − 40| / 40 = 10/40 = 0.25
Categorical part: "blue" ≠ "red" → mismatch = 1
Gower distance = average(0.25, 1) = 0.625

This app takes a different, also-standard route for mixed data: categorical predictor columns get one-hot encoded (one 0/1 column per category) before training, so k-NN's Euclidean distance ends up treating "different category" as a fixed distance, same as any other numeric difference. Gower's distance (above) is a genuine alternative — it scores a categorical mismatch as a fraction of the overall distance rather than folding it into the same Euclidean sum — but isn't what this tool uses.

Lagrangian Duality & KKT Conditions — "finding the widest street between two neighborhoods"

A Support Vector Machine looks for the widest possible "street" that separates two classes, with the decision boundary running down the middle of it. The points that end up sitting exactly on the edges of the street are called support vectors — they're the only points that matter for defining the boundary; if you moved any of the other, farther-away points around, the boundary wouldn't budge.

Finding the widest-margin boundary is a constrained optimization problem: maximize the width of the street, subject to (ideally) every point being correctly placed on its side. The Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT) conditions are the mathematical rules that any correct solution to this kind of problem must satisfy — they're what let you verify a candidate boundary is truly the best one, not just a decent one. Lagrangian duality is the technique classically used to rewrite the original ("primal") version of this problem into an equivalent, often easier-to-solve ("dual") form. This machinery is genuinely graduate-level material — the important takeaway for now is just the "widest street, defined only by the closest points" picture.

Worth knowing: this app's SVM/SVR don't solve that classic dual/KKT optimization directly — they use a simpler gradient-descent-based method (Pegasos, described above) that pursues the same goal (maximize margin / minimize hinge loss) by a different computational route.

Used by: SVMSVR
Handling Imbalanced Binary Data — "when accuracy lies to you"

Imagine a rare-disease dataset where 99% of patients are healthy and only 1% are sick. A lazy model that predicts "healthy" for absolutely everyone gets 99% accuracy — while being completely useless for finding the sick patients it was supposedly built to catch. This is the central danger of imbalanced data: overall accuracy can look excellent while the model has learned nothing useful about the minority class.

Worked example: 990 healthy, 10 sick (1% positive rate). A model that always predicts "healthy": Accuracy = 990/1000 = 99.0%, but Recall on the sick class = 0/10 = 0% — it catches zero actual cases. The headline number hides a completely failed model.

A few of the standard ways to deal with this:

Better metrics: judge the model on precision/recall/F1 for the minority (positive) class specifically — not overall accuracy. Even ROC-AUC can look deceptively reasonable under heavy imbalance since it doesn't weight by class frequency; a Precision-Recall curve is often more revealing once the positive class is rare, since its baseline (unlike the ROC diagonal) actually drops as positives get rarer.

Resampling: oversample the minority class (duplicate minority rows, or synthesize new ones by interpolating between existing minority neighbors — a common technique called SMOTE), or undersample the majority class (randomly drop some majority rows). Oversampling risks overfitting to repeated/synthetic points; undersampling risks throwing away genuinely useful majority-class information.

Class weighting: instead of touching the data, tell the algorithm to penalize mistakes on the minority class more heavily during training, so it can't just ignore the minority class to minimize overall error.

Threshold adjustment: most classifiers output a probability and then apply a default 0.5 cutoff to decide the class. Moving that cutoff — for instance, down toward the minority class's base rate — can rebalance precision and recall for the minority class without retraining anything.

This is exactly why the "Positive Class" selector, per-class metrics, and the Random Classifier baseline exist in this app — comparing your model's minority-class recall against the Random Classifier's is a quick, concrete gut-check for whether it's actually learning the minority class or just riding the majority.

Causal Inference: Uplift Modeling & Propensity Scores — "would this have happened anyway?"

The Lift Chart elsewhere in this app ranks rows by predicted probability and checks how concentrated the actual positives are in the top-ranked group compared to random — that's about how well a model can find likely positives. A related but genuinely different question, common in marketing and policy analysis, is causal: "if we treat this specific person — send them an offer, give them a drug — would that cause a good outcome that wouldn't have happened otherwise?" Answering that requires a different kind of model, often also called "uplift modeling," that estimates each individual's incremental response to treatment — not just their likelihood of a good outcome regardless of treatment.

One common approach — the Two-Model (T-Learner) approach, available as its own "Uplift Modeling" mode in this app — trains two separate models: one on people who received the treatment, one on people who didn't, each predicting probability of the desired outcome. An individual's uplift score is the difference between what the two models predict for them. This lets you target the "persuadables" (people who'd convert only if treated) instead of wasting effort on "sure things" (who'd convert either way), "lost causes" (who never will), or "sleeping dogs" (who'd actually be put off by being targeted).

Worked example: 100 treated customers, 30 converted (30%); 100 untreated customers, 20 converted (20%). The naive overall lift is 30% / 20% = 1.5× — but that's one average number applied to everyone. Uplift modeling instead tries to find which specific customers are driving that 10-percentage-point gap, so a personalized version of that gap can be estimated per individual, rather than assuming every customer benefits from treatment equally.

Propensity scores tackle a related problem in observational (non-randomized) studies: you can't always fairly compare a treated group to an untreated group directly, because who received treatment might not be random — e.g., sicker patients might be more likely to receive a particular drug in the first place, confounding any naive comparison. A propensity score is the estimated probability that a given individual would receive treatment, based on their observed characteristics — often estimated with logistic regression (tying back to the maximum likelihood section above). By comparing individuals with similar propensity scores across the treated and untreated groups, researchers approximate a fairer, more "as-if-randomized" comparison, helping isolate the treatment's actual effect from other differences between who did and didn't receive it.

Worked example: two patients both have an estimated 70% propensity to receive a new drug based on their age, symptoms, and history — but only one of them actually got it. Because their propensity scores match closely, comparing just this pair's outcomes is a far fairer apples-to-apples comparison than comparing the entire group of drug recipients to the entire group of non-recipients, who may differ systematically in other confounding ways.
